ABSTRACT

Numerical search has become a routine instrument of mathematical research: before a conjecture is proved, it is tested against thousands of candidate counterexamples, and a conjecture that survives is often written down as a result. We report a case in which this practice failed, and in which the failure was found only because a human proof attempt and a machine search corrected each other. In a theory of what it costs a decision-maker to learn how its own actions change the data it later learns from, a lower bound says this cost cannot fall below a fixed floor, and we asked whether an adversary who knows the functional form of the response could beat it. An adversarial search over thousands of experimental designs said no, with a best ratio of 1.005, and we recorded the floor as structure-proof. The proof we then wrote established the bound only within two curvature lengths of the operating point; the obstruction was a single inequality that fails outside that region. Searching exactly where the proof stopped produced a counterexample: a four-point design with one far probe of weight 3 × 10^-4, attaining ratio 0.8517, verified at 50-digit precision. The original verdict was a search artifact; the corrected result is two-sided and exact. We describe the loop that made the correction visible and place the episode among eleven such corrections recorded in the same project.

TL;DR: A numerical search declared a lower bound structure-proof; a human proof established it only on a region and named the boundary, and searching just outside that region produced a verified counterexample, turning a false verdict into an exact two-sided theorem.

Keywords: machine-assisted mathematics; human-AI research loop; counterexample search; numerical verification; performative prediction
