\begin{abstract}
A learning agent can satisfy every single-agent stability criterion and still help destabilize the market it operates in. When adaptive agents share an environment that reacts to their decisions, each agent's retraining reshapes the data every other agent learns from next, a \emph{learning externality} that no agent internalizes. Its strength is governed not by the number of firms but by the \emph{effective number of independent learners}, the leading eigenvalue of the correlation matrix of firms' response Jacobians, so foundation-model concentration enters the stability condition as a term rather than as a concern: fifty firms fine-tuning one vendor's model are dynamically close to one large learner. Three closed-form levers follow from a reduction lemma we derive in the main text. A crowding-cadence frontier bounds how often a market can retrain and closes past a critical crowding level. A herd-immunity threshold makes the market stable exactly when the corrected fraction exceeds $(1-1/\mN)/e$ at correction efficacy $e$, the imperfect-vaccine coverage law with the systemic modulus as the reproduction number. A Pigouvian wedge prices the externality, and every market of two or more firms over-adapts. The last two levers are substitutes along a frontier we compute. In a shared-pool order-flow market the mechanism reproduces a published $1.74\times$ and $3.16\times$ amplification at two and three firms, bit for bit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A learning agent can pass every stability test we know how to run and still help destabilize the market it operates in.

The test we know how to run is a single-agent test. A model is evaluated, stress-tested and certified alone, against a distribution treated as given. When the deployed model reshapes that distribution, the performative prediction literature supplies the correction: repeated retraining converges if and only if a modulus $m = \varepsilon\beta/\gamma$ stays below one, where $\varepsilon$ measures how strongly the environment responds and $\gamma$ is the learner's own objective curvature \citep{perdomo2020}. Certify $m<1$ and the loop is safe.

It is not, once the agent has company. Consider dealers whose quotes reshape a common pool of informed flow, recommenders competing for one attention pool, or lenders pricing against one population of borrowers. Each agent's retraining changes the data every \emph{other} agent will learn from next, and no agent internalizes that effect. We call this a \textbf{learning externality}: every participant behaves rationally, every participant passes its own test, and the interaction amplifies a loop none of them chose. The intuition is not new. Banks that each diversify optimally hold similar portfolios and fail together \citep{beale2011,wagner2010}, and funds running similar strategies unwound simultaneously in August 2007 with the crowding invisible from any single risk report \citep{khandani2007}. What is new is that the agents now \emph{learn}, which changes both what determines the danger and what can be done about it.

\paragraph{What determines it is not how many firms there are.}
Let firm $i$ have a response Jacobian $E_i$ describing how its own deployment reshapes the flow it faces, and let $R$ be the correlation matrix of the vectorized $E_i$. We show the market's stability is governed by $\mN = \Neff\, m_1$ with $\Neff = 1 + \kappa(\lmax(R)-1)$, where $\kappa$ is the spillover between firms and $\lmax(R)$ is the \textbf{effective number of independent learners}: a hundred firms perturbing the environment in a hundred orthogonal directions are dynamically one firm. With $s$ the fraction of each response attributable to a common model, vendor or pretraining corpus, this reads $\mN = m_1(1 + \kappa s (N-1))$, so \textbf{the effective number of learners is the number of independent models, not the number of firms.} The quantity a competition regulator measures is the wrong one: fifty equal-share firms have a minimal Herfindahl index and look perfectly competitive, and if all fifty share one vendor they are dynamically a monoculture \citep{hirschman1964}.

\paragraph{What can be done about it takes three forms, and two are substitutes.}
Because every result is stated in $\mN$, each inherits the supply chain for free. \emph{Slow down:} $K$-step retraining faces a window $K < K_{\max}(\mN)$ that narrows as crowding rises and closes past a critical crowding level beyond which no frequency is safe. \emph{Correct:} feedback-aware updates \citep{izzo2021} run inside the market produce exactly the epidemiological herd-immunity law with the systemic modulus as the reproduction number. \emph{Diversify:} that threshold rises in $s$, so a market reaches stability along either axis, and the iso-stability frontier between the two is this paper's most directly usable object. Pricing the externality completes the policy triple, and the decentralized equilibrium over-adapts for every $N \geq 2$.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1) The effective number of independent learners as the reproduction number of a market of adaptive models, derived from a reduction lemma, turning foundation-model concentration into a term in a stability condition, with two counterexamples invalidating mean-similarity diversity indices. (2) The crowding-cadence frontier and its critical crowding level, composing lazy deployment with multi-agent amplification. (3) A mixed-market herd-immunity theorem, exact at finite correction strength, whose clean form is the imperfect-vaccine coverage law and which carries a critical efficacy. (4) The substitution frontier between diversity and corrected learning, which exists only once (1) and (3) are expressed in one quantity. (5) The Pigouvian wedge and the over-adaptation corollary.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Monoculture, and why it is static.}
\citet{kleinberg2021} show that when many firms adopt the same algorithm welfare can fall even when the shared algorithm is the better one, because correlated selection destroys the option value of independent evaluation; \citet{bommasani2022} formalize systemic exclusion when many deployments inherit one model. Both are cross-sectional, measured at one point in time on one cohort, so neither can express a stability boundary or a rate. A market can be statically fine and dynamically fragile at the same shared-model fraction.

\paragraph{Multiplayer performative prediction, and the containment relation.}
\citet{narang2023} is the nearest dynamics: multiple learners in a shared decision-dependent environment, with equilibrium concepts and convergence conditions for repeated retraining. The containment relation is worth stating, since Theorem~\ref{thm:alignment} should not have to be taken on trust as more than a corollary. \textbf{Theirs bounds scalars}: the sensitivity of the joint distribution map to the players' decisions, measured by Lipschitz constants, against their objective curvature, uniformly over the joint action. \textbf{Ours bounds a spectral radius}, $\mN = m_1[1+\kappa(\lmax(R)-1)]$, in which those scalars appear only inside $m_1$. \textbf{Ours is strictly sharper on the class their constants describe, and is not implied by theirs.} Sharper, because at fixed constants $\Neff$ still ranges over $[1,1+\kappa(N-1)]$, so a uniform bound must hold at the worst alignment structure while ours need not, and every market between the two is one their condition rejects and ours admits. Not implied, because nothing in their framework represents \emph{which direction} each agent perturbs the environment: two markets with identical constants and opposite alignment structure are indistinguishable to them and differ in stability for us. The substitution $\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\!\to R$ adds an object rather than tightening a constant, and that object, not the dynamics, carries monoculture, provenance, the supply chain and every policy statement below. The single-learner lazy-deployment slope of \citet{mendlerdunner2020} is what Theorem~\ref{thm:cadence} composes with multi-agent amplification, a composition not previously made; the corrected update of \citet{izzo2021} is what Theorem~\ref{thm:mixed} puts into the game, having only ever been run single-learner.

\paragraph{Systemic risk, epidemiology, and the statistic.}
\citet{beale2011} asked this question of banks, showing individually prudent portfolio choices can be collectively destabilizing; their agents do not learn, so the homogeneity is a portfolio overlap rather than an alignment of feedback directions, and there is no second instrument to trade diversity against. \citet{diekmann1990} converts the epidemiological parallel from analogy into identity: $R_0$ in a heterogeneous population is \emph{defined} as the spectral radius of a next-generation operator, and $\mN$ is the spectral radius of a linearized joint retraining operator, so the two thresholds are the same statement and the vaccination law transfers with its refinements. The largest eigenvalue of a correlation matrix as an effective count is meanwhile standard in random-matrix finance \citep{laloux1999,plerou2002}, signal processing \citep{roy2007} and ecology \citep{hill1973}; the novelty is the matrix it is computed from, response Jacobians rather than returns, and the condition it enters. Work reading macroeconomic state off public sentiment \citep{pebsa2024} estimates through an exogenous channel, whose accuracy does not depend on the state of the system being measured, while the estimator of Section~\ref{sec:limitations} reads an endogenous one that sharpens as the market approaches instability, so the two face opposite sample-complexity problems near the event of interest.

\section{Setup: private against systemic stability}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{The base result.}
Consider $N$ symmetric dealers quoting the same instruments and sharing one pool of informed order flow, coupled by a spillover $\kappa \in [0,1]$: one dealer tightening its quotes raises the toxic flow routed to every dealer, in proportion $\kappa$. Each periodically retrains on the flow it has observed and redeploys, and a firm in isolation is stable exactly when its own performative modulus satisfies $m_1 < 1$. The joint retraining map has Jacobian $J = -m_1[(1-\kappa)I + \kappa \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}]$, whose dominant common mode sits at $-m_1 \Neff$ with $\Neff = 1+\kappa(N-1)$, so the market crosses into instability a factor $\Neff$ earlier than any individual dealer's own loop would. Measured in a shared-pool simulator with real spreads, inventory and liquidity feedback, amplification is $1.74\times$ at $N=2$ and $3.16\times$ at $N=3$ against a linear prediction of $2$ and $3$ \citep{reflex2026}. This is inherited scaffolding; everything below replaces one object in it.

\paragraph{Two questions, and they are not the same question.}
Is each agent stable alone, $m_1 < 1$? And is the collection stable when its members interact, $\mN < 1$? A shared environment separates them, because the loop closes through that environment and nothing in dealer A's validation set contains dealer B. \textbf{Agent count is not a descriptive characteristic of a market; it enters the stability condition.} Read as economics, this is an externality, and it is \emph{technological rather than pecuniary} \citep{buchanan1962}: firms affecting each other only through prices impose no market failure, since the price movement is a transfer, while firms that reshape the distribution their competitors learn from change the production technology of everyone's forecast, which is a genuine efficiency loss. We call $\mN$ the \textbf{feedback reproduction number}: the factor by which a common perturbation is amplified per retraining round.

\paragraph{Objects and standing assumptions.}
Firm $i$ has a response Jacobian $E_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. The \emph{alignment matrix} $R = (r_{ij})$ collects $r_{ij} = \langle \mathrm{vec}\,E_i, \mathrm{vec}\,E_j\rangle / (\|E_i\|_F \|E_j\|_F)$, a correlation matrix of feedback directions rather than of returns; it is positive semidefinite with unit diagonal.

\begin{assumption}[Standing]
\label{ass:standing}
(A1) All spectral statements linearize the joint retraining map around the joint equilibrium, with the simulator as the nonlinear check. (A2) Theorem~\ref{thm:alignment} is exact for equal moduli $m_i = m_1$; unequal moduli give a two-sided bound. (A3) Firms retrain on a common clock at a common cadence $K$. (A4) Firms couple through one pool with a scalar spillover $\kappa$. No sign condition on $R$ is assumed; the one place one is needed is stated where it is used, in Section~\ref{sec:wedge}.
\end{assumption}

\paragraph{The reduction.}
The substitution of $R$ for $\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}$ is the whole paper, so it is derived rather than posited.


\begin{lemma}[Reduction]
\label{lem:reduction}
Assume \textup{(H1)} firm $i$'s deviation from equilibrium is a scalar $x_i$ along its unit response direction $\hat{E}_i = E_i/\|E_i\|_F$, so the state is $x \in \mathbb{R}^N$ rather than $\mathbb{R}^{Nd}$; \textup{(H2)} $\|E_i\|_F = \varepsilon$ for every $i$, which is \textup{(A2)} restated in the new object; \textup{(H3)} firm $i$ retrains against the component of the pool distortion along $\hat{E}_i$, feeling its own contribution in full and each competitor's with weight $\kappa$; and \textup{(H4)} firm $i$ minimizes a $\gamma$-strongly-convex objective with sensitivity $\beta$ to the felt distortion, so $x_i^{+} = -(\beta/\gamma)\,\mathrm{felt}_i$. Then the joint retraining map is linear with Jacobian
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reduction}
J \;=\; -m_1\bigl[(1-\kappa)I + \kappa R\bigr], \qquad m_1 = \varepsilon\beta/\gamma .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The pool carries the aggregate distortion $Z = \sum_j x_j E_j$, so by (H3) firm $i$ feels $\mathrm{felt}_i = \langle \hat{E}_i, x_i E_i\rangle + \kappa\sum_{j\neq i}\langle \hat{E}_i, x_j E_j\rangle$. By (H2), $\langle \hat{E}_i, E_j\rangle = \varepsilon\, r_{ij}$, and since $r_{ii}=1$ the own term folds into the sum, leaving $\mathrm{felt}_i = \varepsilon[\,(1-\kappa)x_i + \kappa (Rx)_i\,]$. Applying (H4) coordinatewise gives \eqref{eq:reduction}, with $m_1$ the single-firm modulus of \citet{perdomo2020}.
\end{proof}

Alignment enters through (H3): a firm senses the pool through its own response channel, so two firms perturbing the environment in orthogonal directions do not read each other at all, however large those perturbations. (H1) is the substantive hypothesis; without it $J$ carries a Kronecker structure on $\mathbb{R}^{Nd}$ whose radius is not in general $\Neff m_1$, the block-secular problem deferred in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Result 1: the effective number of independent learners}
\label{sec:alignment}

Counting firms is wrong, and the correction is where monoculture and supply-chain content enter. $\lmax(R)$ runs from $1$, all responses orthogonal, to $N$, monoculture.

\begin{theorem}[Alignment sets the reproduction number]
\label{thm:alignment}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:standing} with equal moduli and heterogeneous response directions, the joint Jacobian is \eqref{eq:reduction} and the market's spectral radius is
\begin{equation}
\mN \;=\; \Neff\, m_1, \qquad \Neff \;=\; 1 + \kappa\bigl(\lmax(R) - 1\bigr),
\end{equation}
so the market is stable if and only if $\mN < 1$.
\end{theorem}

Setting $R = \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}$ recovers the symmetric multi-dealer law of \citet{reflex2026} exactly, so the published result is not generalized away but located: it is the monoculture corner, where every firm's feedback points the same way. Four anchors follow, each checked independently (Table~\ref{tab:anchors}). $\Neff \geq 1$ always, so interaction never stabilizes a market below what its members achieve alone.

\paragraph{The supply chain.}
Decompose $E_i = \sqrt{s}\,E_{\text{shared}} + \sqrt{1-s}\,\Xi_i$, where $s\in[0,1]$ is the fraction attributable to a shared foundation model, vendor or pretraining corpus. Alignments concentrate at $r_{ij}\to s$ with $O(1/d)$ fluctuation and an explicit failure probability, so $\Neff \to 1 + \kappa s (N-1)$ and every result below inherits $s$ for free: fifty dealers fine-tuning one vendor's model are dynamically ${\sim}1+49\kappa$ learners at $s\approx 1$ and barely more than one at $s \approx 0$.

\paragraph{Why the naive diversity index fails, twice.}
Mean alignment understates \emph{clustered} alignment: three tightly aligned firms among ten otherwise-orthogonal ones give $\Neff = 2.60$ at $\kappa=0.8$ against $1.48$ for the uniform configuration with the identical mean, so a market at $m_1 = 0.5$ runs $\mN = 1.30$ and is unstable while the mean index reports $0.74$ and calls it safe with margin. The simplex is sharper: it minimizes mean alignment at $-1/(N-1)$ yet its $\lmax = N/(N-1)$ exceeds orthogonality's $1$, so the mean \emph{does not order configurations correctly} and fails as a ranking, not only as a level. The failure is signed: $\Neff \geq 1 + \kappa(N-1)\overline{r}$ for every $R$, so the mean index always under-states risk.

\section{Result 2: the crowding-cadence frontier}
\label{sec:cadence}

The first lever is quantity regulation, and it is the one a firm can pull alone: retrain less often. A learner taking only $K$ gradient steps per deployment realizes outer-map slope $\mu(K) = -m + c^K(1+m)$, where $c = 1-\eta\gamma$ is the inner per-step contraction \citep{mendlerdunner2020}. Because $c$ is built from the learner's own objective curvature and step size, it does not depend on how many competitors it has, and that independence is what makes the composition below work. We verify it by measuring the realized contraction inside the joint market.

\begin{theorem}[Systemic cadence window]
\label{thm:cadence}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:standing}, in the $N$-firm market with synchronous $K$-step retraining the joint deployment map is $c^K I + (1-c^K)J$, so the binding mode has slope $\mu_N(K) = -\mN + c^K(1+\mN)$ and the market is stable if and only if
\begin{equation}
K \;<\; K_{\max} \;=\; \frac{\ln\bigl((\mN-1)/(\mN+1)\bigr)}{\ln c},
\end{equation}
with $K_{\max}$ infinite when $\mN \le 1$ and strictly decreasing in $\mN$.
\end{theorem}

The slope on the mode carrying $\lambda_i(R)$ is strictly decreasing in $\lambda_i$, which puts every slope at or below $c^K<1$, so the upper side of the constraint never binds at any cadence, and places the binding mode at $\lmax$ whatever the sign pattern of $R$.

\paragraph{Critical crowding.}
The same inequality reads $\mN < (1+c^K)/(1-c^K)$, and since $K$ is a positive integer the most permissive case is $K=1$. A stabilizing cadence exists at all if and only if $\mN < (1+c)/(1-c)$, a factor of $9$ at $c=0.8$. Past that the market is unstable at every retraining frequency. The laziest retrainer is therefore the hardest to destabilize.

\paragraph{What the supply chain does to the window.}
Substituting Result~1 makes $K_{\max}$ a function of vendor concentration at a fixed number of firms: at $m_1 = 0.15$, $\kappa = 0.8$, $N=30$, $c=0.8$, the window runs $K_{\max} = 20.68$ at $s = 0.25$, $5.28$ at $s=0.5$ and $2.53$ at $s=1$. Holding competitors fixed and raising vendor concentration cuts every incumbent's retraining budget by a factor of eight. The externality in its sharpest form is therefore not merely that \textbf{your competitor's entry consumes your retraining budget} but that \textbf{your competitor's choice of vendor does too}, without their entering at all.

\paragraph{What the lever costs.}
Cadence buys stability with staleness, and the exchange rate is the same quantity on both sides: after a retraining round the deployed model's gap to its own best response is exactly $c^K$ times what it was, so the $c^K$ that widens the stability margin is the staleness.

\section{Result 3: herd immunity, and diversity as its substitute}
\label{sec:herd}

The second lever is a technology mandate, and unlike cadence it is not something a firm can pull alone to any effect. A \emph{corrected} firm runs the feedback-aware update \citep{izzo2021,miller2021}, so its retraining is governed by the objective curvature $\gamma_{\mathrm{PO}}$ in place of the cobweb and its modulus is scaled by $\theta = \gamma/\gamma_{\mathrm{PO}} < 1$. What is new is running it inside the game: a mixed market of $N_b$ blind firms and $N - N_b$ corrected ones, corrected fraction $\rho = 1 - N_b/N$. Correction changes a firm's gain and not its response direction, so $R$ is untouched.

\begin{theorem}[Mixed-market stability]
\label{thm:mixed}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:standing}, with supply-chain alignment and two correction levels, the joint Jacobian's symmetric congruence is a diagonal matrix plus a positive rank-one term, so its spectrum solves a secular equation. Two distinct moduli collapse it to a quadratic, and for $1\le N_b \le N-1$ the radius is its larger root,
\begin{equation}
\rho(J) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(P + \sqrt{P^2 - 4Q}\bigr),\quad
P = (1-\tilde{\kappa})m_1(1+\theta) + \tilde{\kappa} m_1(N_b + \theta N_c),\quad
Q = \theta m_1^2 (1-\tilde{\kappa})\Neff,
\end{equation}
with $\tilde{\kappa} = \kappa s$ and $N_c = N - N_b$. An empty block needs the single-block form instead, the quadratic otherwise leaving a phantom root that can exceed the true radius.
\end{theorem}

$\Neff$ appearing inside $Q$ is why every statement below carries $s$: the mixed market inherits Result~1's crowding through the product term, not by stipulation.

\paragraph{The strong-correction limit, and it errs in the unsafe direction.}
As $\gamma_{\mathrm{PO}}\to\infty$ the constant term vanishes and the unstable cycle runs through blind firms only, giving $N_b < N_c(s) = 1 + (1/m_1 - 1)/(\kappa s)$. That criterion taken alone is \textbf{optimistic rather than conservative}. The radius is nondecreasing in $\theta$ by Perron-Frobenius on an entrywise-nonnegative matrix, so the limit under-states it: on random draws it calls $11.8\%$ of configurations stable that are unstable at every finite correction strength. The exact root is therefore the criterion and the limit its corollary, quoted with its error direction. The same monotonicity carries the reassuring half: \textbf{correction never backfires}, so no configuration exists in which un-blinding a firm destabilizes the market.

\paragraph{The collapse, and its honest generalization.}
At $\kappa = s = 1$ the limit reads $\rho^{*} = 1 - 1/\mN$, \textbf{exactly the epidemiological herd-immunity threshold $1-1/R_0$} with the systemic modulus as the reproduction number. A market of 10 firms at $\mN = 2.5$ needs 60\% of them un-blinded \emph{if correction is perfect}. It is not, and at that same corner the quadratic degenerates and the stability condition solves exactly for
\begin{equation}
\rho^{*}(e) \;=\; \frac{1 - 1/\mN}{e}, \qquad e \;=\; 1 - \gamma/\gamma_{\mathrm{PO}},
\end{equation}
with $e$ the \textbf{correction efficacy}. That is the standard coverage requirement for an \emph{imperfect vaccine}, of which the clean law is the perfect-efficacy corner. The clean form assumes homogeneous mixing \citep{fine2011}, and the collapse here assumes the $\kappa=s=1$ corner for precisely that reason.

\begin{corollary}[Critical efficacy]
\label{cor:efficacy}
$\rho^{*}(e)$ exceeds one, so no corrected fraction works at all, exactly when $\theta > 1/\mN$. Correction is a usable lever only if $\gamma_{\mathrm{PO}}/\gamma > \mN$.
\end{corollary}

At $\mN = 2.5$ the corrected update must deliver a $2.5\times$ curvature improvement or un-blinding cannot stabilize the market whatever fraction is treated. This parallels Section~\ref{sec:cadence}'s critical crowding: each lever has a regime past which it stops working, and knowing both is what makes trading them off honest rather than an extrapolation.

\paragraph{The synthesis.}
$\rho^{*}$ is increasing in $s$, strictly wherever positive, so a market reaches stability along either axis. At $N=20$, $m_1 = 0.15$, $\kappa = 0.8$: a monoculture ($s=1$) needs $\rho^{*} = 0.596$, that is $12$ corrected firms; at $s = 0.5$, $\rho^{*} = 0.242$, five firms; at $s = 0.2$, $\rho^{*} = 0$, so the market is stable with no corrected agents at all. The regulator's count is $N - \lceil N_c \rceil + 1$ rather than $\lceil \rho^{*}N \rceil$, and the two differ by a firm exactly when $N_c$ is an integer.

\paragraph{The economic reading.}
Correction is a public good: an un-blinded firm captures a private benefit while the stability it contributes accrues to everyone, so the market free-rides below the threshold and does not reach $\rho^{*}$ unaided \citep{bergstrom1986}. Model diversity has the same structure, a firm choosing an idiosyncratic vendor paying privately and supplying systemic stability for free. \textbf{The substitution is therefore between two goods that are both under-supplied}, not between a good and a bad.

\section{Result 4: the Pigouvian wedge}
\label{sec:wedge}

The third lever is the price instrument. Inside the stable region the binding mode is an AR(1) with coefficient $-\mN$ driven by noise of variance $\sigma^2$, so its stationary variance is $V(\mN) = \sigma^2/(1-\mN^2)$: the smooth proxy for divergence risk, divergent exactly at the boundary the rest of the paper is about, and nothing below uses more than $V$ being positive, increasing and convex. The sign the variance does not see is not lost: the lag-1 autocorrelation is $-\mN$ and therefore \emph{negative}, so a crowded market's common mode oscillates rather than persists, which is the observable the supervision estimator of Section~\ref{sec:limitations} reads.

Each firm picks an adaptation aggressiveness $a_i$, meaning cadence, learning rate or responsiveness, buying concave tracking benefit $B(a_i)$ through $m_i = \mu(a_i)$. Firm $i$ bears weight $w_i$ on common-mode variance and the planner $W$, with $W > \sum_i w_i$ because every trade has a client bearing execution-quality variance that no dealer's objective contains. That is an assumption, and the results turn on its \emph{sign} and never its size.

\begin{lemma}[Marginal crowding share]
\label{lem:share}
At the symmetric point, $\partial \mN/\partial m_i = \Neff v_i^2$ where $v$ is the leading eigenvector of the coupling matrix. The shares sum to $\Neff$ rather than to one, and on exchangeable $R$ each equals $\Neff/N$.
\end{lemma}

\paragraph{Where a sign condition is needed, and where it is not.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:alignment}'s radius needs none: $R$ is positive semidefinite with unit diagonal, so every $\lambda_i(R)\ge 0$ and $1+\kappa(\lambda_i-1)$ increases in $\lambda_i$, placing the radius at $\lmax$ whatever the off-diagonal signs. What needs one is the \emph{reading} of Lemma~\ref{lem:share}, that the binding mode is the common one so $v$ is a vector of market shares. \textbf{(A5)} supplies it: with $R$ entrywise nonnegative, Perron-Frobenius places the leading eigenvector in the nonnegative orthant, and shared vendors and corpora produce positive alignment.

Both readings of Lemma~\ref{lem:share} matter. The \emph{sum} is $\Neff$, so the market's total sensitivity to a uniform increase in aggressiveness is amplified by exactly the effective learner count of Result~1. The \emph{individual} share is $\Neff/N$, the arithmetic shape of every commons problem. The naive guess here is $1/N$ by analogy with the commons, and it is wrong by the factor $\Neff$, which at $N=20$, $\kappa=0.8$, $s=1$ is $16.2$.

\begin{theorem}[The wedge]
\label{thm:wedge}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:standing} and the welfare assumptions above, at a symmetric profile the private and social first-order conditions are the same equation with a different price on crowding, and the marginal cost a firm ignores is
\begin{equation}
t^{*} \;=\; (W - w_i)\, V'(\mN)\, \frac{\Neff}{N}\, \mu'(a), \qquad V'(\mN) = \frac{2\sigma^2 \mN}{(1-\mN^2)^2}.
\end{equation}
A per-unit fee $t^{*}$ on aggressiveness makes the private condition coincide with the social one, so the taxed decentralized equilibrium implements the symmetric social optimum.
\end{theorem}

With symmetric weights the private condition ignores the fraction $(N-1)/N$ of the marginal variance cost borne by other firms, plus the entire client-side exposure. $t^{*}$ is strictly increasing in $N$, $\kappa$, $s$ and $\mN$, and diverges at the boundary like $(1-\mN)^{-2}$. The rate is the economically loaded part: a regulator setting a fee from a comfortable-looking market and holding it fixed is setting it far too low by the time the market is close to instability.

\paragraph{The provenance channel.}
$\mN$ depends on $s$, so the wedge has a second channel: $\partial\mN/\partial a_i = (\Neff/N)\mu'(a_i)$ against $\partial \mN/\partial s = m_1\kappa(N-1)$. The same wedge therefore prices \textbf{shared-model adoption} directly, turning the monoculture externality into a fee rather than a warning, and the two channels are asymmetric. The aggressiveness factor $\Neff/N$ is bounded and tends to $\kappa$ from above, so a firm's marginal footprint through its own cadence does not grow without bound, while the provenance channel is \textbf{linear in $N$ and does not decay} against a private adoption gain that does not grow with market size at all. Unregulated adoption therefore runs toward the unstable configuration, which is why the diversity floor of Section~\ref{sec:herd} is a different instrument from the cadence cap of Section~\ref{sec:cadence} rather than a restatement of it.

\begin{corollary}[Over-adaptation]
\label{cor:over}
The decentralized equilibrium over-adapts relative to the social optimum for every $N \geq 2$, strictly so even with client exposure switched off, and the distortion grows as the market crowds. At $N=1$ with no client exposure the wedge is exactly zero.
\end{corollary}

The fraction the private condition ignores is free of $\Neff$: with no client exposure it is exactly $(N-1)/N$ whatever $\kappa$ and $s$ are. Crowding enters the wedge's \emph{level}, through $V'(\mN)$, and not the distortion's \emph{shape}, so Corollary~\ref{cor:over} is the generic commons result and the provenance asymmetry above is the market-of-models one. Sections~\ref{sec:cadence}, \ref{sec:herd} and \ref{sec:wedge} are respectively quantity regulation, a technology mandate or structural remedy, and the price instrument; choosing among them under uncertainty is Weitzman's question \citep{weitzman1974}, and we supply the objects needed to pose it rather than answering it.

\paragraph{The one measured panel.}
Panel 1 reproduces a prior published run in that run's own simulator, at the relative error Table~\ref{tab:panels} records. \textbf{The gap to the linear prediction is content, not error}: the market runs $-12.9\%$ at $N=2$ and $+5.2\%$ at $N=3$, with \emph{opposite signs}, which is what a nonlinear market with saturating flow does around a linearization. The differential mode measures $3.4\times 10^{-3}$ against a theoretical zero at $\kappa=1$, so the instability is purely common-mode, the mechanism Theorem~\ref{thm:alignment} generalizes.

\paragraph{Why the rest are not measured.}

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Supervision from public prices.}
Every quantity above is hidden from the regulator these results address, and near the boundary the system tells on itself: as $\mN$ approaches one, the leading principal component of public quotes has lag-1 autocorrelation approaching one in magnitude, negative because the common mode oscillates, and variance share growing like $1/(1-\mN)$. A supervisor can therefore estimate distance to instability from public prices alone, and the estimate sharpens as the market nears the boundary it watches for. Its consistency proof, sample complexity and real-data panel are deferred, the last scoped in the appendix as consistency evidence with a placebo, never identification.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The analysis is linearized around the joint equilibrium (A1), and panel 1 shows what that costs in a real market. \textbf{The heterogeneous sweep is closed-form agreement, not measurement}, the largest gap in the work; routing the liquidity and impact channels through the response profiles is the identified repair. Theorem~\ref{thm:alignment} is exact for equal moduli (A2), unequal ones giving a two-sided bound, and share-weighted alignment, the block-secular reduction when (H1) fails, and the sharp concentration rate are named extensions. The $1-1/R_0$ collapse assumes the $\kappa=s=1$ corner, three or more correction levels return a genuine secular equation, and correction moving a response direction rather than a gain is not modelled. Deployment is synchronous (A3); heterogeneous clocks break the eigenvector sharing Theorem~\ref{thm:cadence} uses. Theorem~\ref{thm:wedge} prices instability through stationary variance, a modeling choice. Neither $R$ nor $s$ is observable to an outsider, which the estimator above answers with its real-data leg deferred.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
\textbf{Individual model evaluation cannot certify a market of models.} The quantity separating the two questions is the effective number of independent learners, which no evaluation of a single model can see: a firm can pass every stability test available to it and still be one of the reasons its market fails. Regulation of shared general-purpose models has thresholds on model scale but no mechanism tying model sharing to a measurable systemic quantity. This paper supplies one, with two substitutable instruments and a price.
